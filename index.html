Publications

In Search of a Unicorn: Dynamic Agency with Endogenous Investment Opportunities

with Felix Feng and Beatrice Michaeli

Journal of Accounting and Economics, 2024

We study the optimal dynamic contract that provides incentives for an agent (e.g., SPAC sponsor, VC general partner, CTO) to exploit investment opportunities/targets that arrive randomly over time via a costly search process. The agent is privy to the arrival as well as to the quality of the target and can take advantage of this for rent extraction during the search process and the ensuing production. The optimal contract provides the agent with incentives for timely and truthful reporting via a time-varying threshold for investment and an internal charge for the time spent on search. In the equilibrium, as time elapses, the charge becomes progressively higher while the investment threshold is progressively lower, resulting in overinvestment at a time-varying degree. Our model generates empirically testable predictions regarding investments (such as M&As, hedge fund activism, VC investing, SPACs, and internal innovations), linking the degree of overinvestment to observable firm and industry characteristics.

Working Papers

Misallocation in Banking

Solo-authored · Preliminary draft available upon request

Bank competition can change not only the rates customers face but which banks hold deposits and make loans. We study the allocative consequences of uneven market power across banks. Because deposits and loans are linked through customer demand and bank balance sheets, whether banking activity is located at the right institutions cannot be assessed from either side alone. We develop a welfare framework that holds aggregate deposits and loans fixed and measures the gains from reallocating deposits and loans through repricing toward banks where an additional dollar creates greater social surplus. We structurally estimate the model using U.S. bank data from 1986 through 2021 and find that the annual welfare loss from market-power-driven misallocation ranged from 10 to 30 percent of banks' total net income in the 1980s and 1990s and declined sharply in recent years. The loan side contributes more to the loss, while the interaction offsets a substantial share because the efficient allocation often expands one side of a bank and contracts the other. Surviving acquirers move farther from the efficient allocation after realized mergers and contribute more to measured misallocation. Freezing the structural estimates in 2021, we also find that subsequent deposit reallocation increasingly runs against the model-implied efficient direction while loan reallocation runs with it. Overall, the results reveal an allocative dimension of bank competition that average rates, spreads, concentration, and one-sided analyses miss.

The Optimal Schedules of Incentives and Cash Flows

with Felix Feng and Mark Westerfield

We model delegated management when the agent can either invest in durable asset quality or raise instantaneous cash flow. Both actions add value, but the principal can only observe aggregate output, not inputs or asset quality, so a schedule of incentives for investment in durable quality also raises instantaneous cash flows. The schedule's flexibility makes long-term incentives relatively cheap, until the contract becomes constrained after a sufficiently long history of negative cash flow surprises. Optimal contracts begin with weak, long-term-focused incentives, and alternate between those and stronger, present-focused incentives. All contracts induce short-term thinking as part of the equilibrium path.

Does Investor Composition Matter for Funds?

with Hossein Poorvasei · Preliminary draft available upon request

We study how the composition of a fund’s investor base relates to performance and capital stability. Our setting is U.S. equity separately managed accounts (SMAs), where institutional clients are observed by type and the small number of accounts makes investor composition salient. We construct a client-type Herfindahl index (CHHI) from the distribution of assets across investor types. Funds with more concentrated client bases subsequently earn higher risk-adjusted returns: the most concentrated portfolio earns about 131 basis points per year more in gross six-factor alpha than the least concentrated portfolio and 96 basis points more net of fees. Concentrated funds also have more stable capital, especially on the downside: their flows respond less to poor performance, extreme outflows occur less often, and negative-flow volatility is lower. After good performance, high-CHHI funds add accounts and client types more slowly and maintain more stable factor exposures. We extend Berk and Green (2004) with relationship-specific capital and a manager capacity choice. Incumbent mandate adjustment costs generate the downside flow pattern. When the value of preserving performance on concentrated incumbent relationships is sufficiently large relative to fee revenue from new assets, managers accept less new capital after good performance and remain below the scale at which alpha is dissipated. The joint return and client-growth evidence is consistent with this capacity-preservation margin being economically meaningful. The empirical design establishes a robust cross-sectional relation and leaves causal identification open.
