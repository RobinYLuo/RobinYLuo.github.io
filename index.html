Publications

In Search of a Unicorn: Dynamic Agency with Endogenous Investment Opportunities

with Felix Feng and Beatrice Michaeli

Journal of Accounting and Economics, 2024

We study the optimal dynamic contract that provides incentives for an agent (e.g., SPAC sponsor, VC general partner, CTO) to exploit investment opportunities/targets that arrive randomly over time via a costly search process. The agent is privy to the arrival as well as to the quality of the target and can take advantage of this for rent extraction during the search process and the ensuing production. The optimal contract provides the agent with incentives for timely and truthful reporting via a time-varying threshold for investment and an internal charge for the time spent on search. In the equilibrium, as time elapses, the charge becomes progressively higher while the investment threshold is progressively lower, resulting in overinvestment at a time-varying degree. Our model generates empirically testable predictions regarding investments (such as M&As, hedge fund activism, VC investing, SPACs, and internal innovations), linking the degree of overinvestment to observable firm and industry characteristics.

Working Papers

Misallocation in Banking

Solo-authored · Preliminary draft available upon request

This paper studies how market power distorts the allocation of deposits and loans across banks. Banking complicates this problem because activity on the two sides of bank balance sheets is connected through both customer demand and bank costs. Demand complementarity raises the social value of placing deposits and loans at the same bank, while cost complementarity changes which banks can jointly supply them most cheaply. We develop and structurally estimate a two-sided oligopolistic banking model that measures the welfare gains from reallocating fixed aggregate deposits and loans across institutions. Because customer demand and bank balance sheets link the two products, the efficient allocation equalizes marginal social surplus on both sides jointly. Using U.S. bank data from 1986 through 2021, we estimate annual welfare losses of \$1.3 to \$3.3 billion in 2019 dollars, equal on average to 5.2 percent of sample banks' net income and 27.6 percent at the peak. The loan side contributes more, while the deposit-loan interaction offsets a substantial share because efficient reallocation often expands one side of a bank and contracts the other. After realized mergers, surviving acquirers move farther from the efficient marginal-surplus benchmarks, yet the combined parties' normalized bank-year welfare loss declines. These findings coexist because a merger affects both the wedges and how costly those wedges are for welfare through balance-sheet quantities and reallocation sensitivity. Freezing the structural estimates in 2021, we also find that subsequent deposit reallocation increasingly runs against the model-implied efficient direction while loan reallocation runs with it. The framework provides a unified way to measure allocative efficiency in two-sided banking and extends beyond bank market power to other sources of marginal-surplus dispersion, such as cost wedges across firms, and to other industries in which demand or production links multiple products.

The Optimal Schedules of Incentives and Cash Flows

with Felix Feng and Mark Westerfield

We model delegated management when the agent can either invest in durable asset quality or raise instantaneous cash flow. Both actions add value, but the principal can only observe aggregate output, not inputs or asset quality, so a schedule of incentives for investment in durable quality also raises instantaneous cash flows. The schedule's flexibility makes long-term incentives relatively cheap, until the contract becomes constrained after a sufficiently long history of negative cash flow surprises. Optimal contracts begin with weak, long-term-focused incentives, and alternate between those and stronger, present-focused incentives. All contracts induce short-term thinking as part of the equilibrium path.

Does Investor Composition Matter for Funds?

with Hossein Poorvasei · Preliminary draft available upon request

We study how the composition of a fund’s investor base relates to performance and capital stability. Our setting is U.S. equity separately managed accounts (SMAs), where institutional clients are observed by type and the small number of accounts makes investor composition salient. We construct a client-type Herfindahl index (CHHI) from the distribution of assets across investor types. Funds with more concentrated client bases subsequently earn higher risk-adjusted returns: the most concentrated portfolio earns about 131 basis points per year more in gross six-factor alpha than the least concentrated portfolio and 96 basis points more net of fees. Concentrated funds also have more stable capital, especially on the downside: their flows respond less to poor performance, extreme outflows occur less often, and negative-flow volatility is lower. After good performance, high-CHHI funds add accounts and client types more slowly and maintain more stable factor exposures. We extend Berk and Green (2004) with relationship-specific capital and a manager capacity choice. Incumbent mandate adjustment costs generate the downside flow pattern. When the value of preserving performance on concentrated incumbent relationships is sufficiently large relative to fee revenue from new assets, managers accept less new capital after good performance and remain below the scale at which alpha is dissipated. The joint return and client-growth evidence is consistent with this capacity-preservation margin being economically meaningful.
